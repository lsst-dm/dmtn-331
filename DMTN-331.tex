\setDocAbstract{%
Background matching is a family of algorithms for estimating the temporal backgrounds of astronomical images by using the differences between pairs of visits as the input data, since the vast majority of astrophysical sources disappear in those differences.  For $N$ images with $N$ different backgrounds, this only yields $N-1$ constraints, ensuring an underconstrained problem.  This technical note sketches out an approach that explicitly projects out the unconstrained linear combination of background models to be fit later after building a coadd, in order to avoid the problem of stitching together a reference image that that complicates most other background-matching methods.
}

\section{Problem}

Given $N$ dithered overlapping visits, we wish to estimate their non-astrophysical backgrounds.
If we first warp all images to a common coordinate system (hereafter \emph{tract} coordinates), we can subtract pairs of images to remove nearly all astrophysical objects and hence provide a much cleaner image for background estimation, but this yields only $N-1$ constraints for $N$ background models.
This means any realistically flexible background model will only be partially constrained by these difference pairs, and the goal of background matching is to be able to estimate this remainder on a coadd, where the signal-to-noise ratio of the astrophysical objects is higher.

The standard approach is to select one visit as a \emph{reference image}, and defining all other backgrounds to it, such that a coadd built with those other backgrounds subtracted yields a coadd with just the reference image's background left in it.
This approach runs into trouble at the edges of that reference image, leading to generalizations that involve somehow stitch together a continuous reference image from multiple visits heuristically.

This technote attempts to avoid this by instead providing a rigorous formalism for background matching that does not give any particular visit special treatment.

\section{Theory}

Let $z_k$ be the warped pixel data for all visits in some superpixel $k$ defined in tract coordinates, as flattened, concatenated vector (i.e. row-blocks of $z_k$ correspond to differen visits), and the corresponding $V_k$ noise variance matrix (assumed diagonal).
We will model the background via some linear model (e.g. splines or polynomials) that maps parameters $\theta$ (also concatenated over all visits) to pixel values via a matrix $J_k$, such that the background-subtracted images for that superpixels are $z_k - J_k\theta$.
Note that the rows of $J_k$ that correspond to one visit may be very different from the rows that correspond to another; this allows models to include amplifier pedestals, illumination corrections, or other basis functions that are defined most naturally in detector or focal-plane coordinates and have to be warped to tract coordinates.

The data vector for background matching is comprised of binned differences between pairs of exposures, which we will represent via per-superpixel matrices $E_k$ whose rows sum the pixels from one visit and subtract the sum of the exact same pixels from another (pixels missing in either visit are ignored in both).
Each row of $E_k$ thus corresponds to one pairwise difference, while the columns correspond to the elements of $z_k$.
Elements of $E_k$ are $0$, $-1$, or $1$.

The negative log likelihood for the parameters $\theta$ given our binned-difference data points is thus
\begin{align}
	\chi^2 & = \frac{1}{2} \sum_k r_k^T C_k^{-1} r_k \label{eqn:chi2_r} \\
	r_k    & \equiv E_k \left( z_k - J_k\theta \right)                  \\
	C_k    & \equiv E_k V_k E_k^T
\end{align}
As long as the number of rows of $E_k$ is less than the number of visits $N_k$ overlapping that superpixel, $C_k$ should be nonsingular.
If more pairs are included, a pseudoinverse can safely be used instead of a true inverse in (\ref{eqn:chi2_r}), as this effectively zeros out the contributions from linear combinations of difference pairs that are degenerate.
This may be useful for cases where the union of the sets of missing pixels of a pair is much larger than the set of missing pixels in either visit.
When all difference pairs in a superpixel have at least one visit with full coverage, there should be no gain from including more than $N_k-1$ pairs.
Note that data points from different superpixels are strictly independent, which is what motivates writing the likelihood as a sum over superpixels (instead of e.g. visits or visit pairs).

The series expansion of $\chi^2$ about $\theta=0$ is thus
\begin{align}
	\chi^2 & = \frac{1}{2} \sum_k z_k^T E_k^T C_k^{-1} E_k z_k
	+ g^T \theta + \frac{1}{2}\theta^T H \theta                \\
	g      & \equiv - \sum_k J_k^T E_k^T C_k^{-1} E_k z_k      \\
	H      & \equiv - \sum_k J_k^T E_k^T C_k^{-1} E_k J_k
\end{align}
and the maximum-likelihood solution for $\theta$ is given by solving
\begin{align}
	H\theta = -g
\end{align}

Unfortunately, $H$ is in general rank-deficient (semidefinite).
This requires a rank-revealing factorization of $H$, and here we will use a column-pivoting QR factorization of $H^T$:
\begin{align}
	H = Q R P^T
\end{align}
where $Q$ is orthogonal, $R$ is upper trapezoidal, and $P$ is a permutation matrix.

Because H is symmetric, we can also write this as a lower-trapezoidal factorization, which we will do in block form:
\begin{align}
	H & = P R^T Q^T \\
	  & =
	\left[
		\begin{array}{c c}
			P_L & P_G
		\end{array}
		\right]
	\left[
		\begin{array}{c c}
			L & 0 \\
			G & 0
		\end{array}
		\right]
	\left[
		\begin{array}{c}
			Q_\mu^T \\
			Q_\nu^T
		\end{array}
	\right]         \\
	  & =
	\left(P_L L + P_F F\right) Q_\mu^T
\end{align}
where $L$ is square and lower triangular and $G$ is rectangular with no special structure.

We can now change variables from $\theta$ to $\mu, \nu$ via
\begin{align}
	\mu    & \equiv Q_\mu^T \theta   \\
	\nu    & \equiv Q_\nu^T \theta   \\
	\theta & = Q_\mu \mu + Q_\nu \nu
\end{align}
and instead solve for $\mu$
\begin{align}
	\left(P_L L + P_G G\right)\mu = g
\end{align}
$Q_\nu$ thus provides a projection onto the null space of $H$: $\nu$ is not constrained at all by the pairwise differences.

By multiplying on the left by $P_L^T$, we can solve for $\mu$ with backsubstitution:
\begin{align}
	\hat{\mu} & = L^{-1} P_L^T g \\
\end{align}
Note that we do not need $G$ or $P_G$ at all.

Letting $\nu = 0$ for now, we can now construct a background-subtracted coadd, by summing background-subtracted images with per-superpixel weight matrices $W_k$ that simply adds all per-visit contributions to a pixel with weights that sum to unity\footnote{The superpixel structure is no longer important at this point, but reusing it saves us from having to change notation.}
\begin{align}
	s_k & = W_k \left( z_k - J_k Q_\mu \hat{\mu} \right)
\end{align}

The problem is now to constrain $\nu$ on this coadd using traditional background subtraction methods, e.g. masking out astrophysical sources as well as we can and binning with some robust statistic.
Instead of interpolating those binned statistics directly, however, we now want to fit the unconstrained degrees of freedom to them.
To do that, we need to also coadd the linear combination of basis functions projected out by $Q_\nu$ with the same weights:
\begin{align}
	B_k & = W_k J_k Q_\nu
\end{align}
We can then minimize the difference between our vector of robust statistics and the predictions from $B_k\nu$ on all superpixels.
For example, in the limit where we compute robust means on the coadd in the same superpixels used earlier, we can correspondingly compute the mean of each row of each $B_k$, and then solve the resulting linear least squares problem for $\nu$.

The chief problem with this formalism is that it naively involves an extremely large matrix; if we wish to fit a smooth background on scales of $1$ degree, using spline functions with knots approximately $20$ arcseconds apart, we have $180 \times 180 = 32400$ free parameters per visit.
With only $20$ visits, $H$ is a $648000 \times 648000$ matrix.

When the background basis functions have limited spatial support, however, $H$ is sparse, and this is a reason to strongly prefer splines over polynomials.
Sparse rank-revealing QR factorizations are readily available (e.g. from the \texttt{Eigen} C++ library).

\section{Concrete basis functions}

\subsection{Tract-coordinate splines}

We have generally assumed throughout this note so far that smooth background variation would be fit via a spline basis, as splines can be configured to be arbitrarily flexible while having spatial limited support (which leads to a sparser $H$ matrix).
While it would be possible to define the spline basis in detector coordinates, there is no real advantage to doing so, as this would make the basis functions more expensive to evaluate in tract coordinates without improving their ability to fit smooth features on the scales we want.
With the splines in tract coordinates, we can evaluate them once in advance for each superpixel, and just adjust the binning for the missing pixels of each visit pair.
Since the support of each spline basis function is easy to reason about, we can also efficiently skip the basis functions that are irrelevant for a superpixel (which is the vast majority).
The advantage of using a tract-coordinate basis set is most pronounced when fitting the unconstrained linear combination $\nu$ on the coadd, as it saves us from actually having to coadd those basis functions at all.

\subsection{Including the background-to-photometric ratio}

Flattening an image for accurate photometry does not necessarily flatten it ideally for background subtraction.
We represent this in the LSST pipelines via a \emph{background to photometric  ratio} calibration image, represented here as a per-superpixel diagonal matrix $F_k$ (just like $V_k$).
Each detector image should be multiplied by this ratio to flatten the background before traditional background subtraction, and then divided by it after the background has been subtracted.
Because we want to build a coadd from images that are photometrically flat, for background matching we instead need to move this ratio into the background model basis:
\begin{align}
	F_k^{-1}\left(F_k z_k - J_k \theta\right) = z_k - F_k^{-1} J_k \theta
\end{align}
i.e. we should substitute
\begin{align}
	J_k \to F_k^{-1} J_k
\end{align}
$F_k$ is most naturally defined in detector coordinates, not the tract pixel grid we will use for background matching.
This means we should warp its \emph{inverse} to tract coordinates for use in scaling the background model basis.

Unfortunately, scaling by the ratio negates many of the efficiency advantages of tract-coordinate basis, as the product will be visit-dependent.

\subsection{Amplifier pedestals}

Small instabilities in gains and other uncharacterized per-amplifier features can lead to background offsets between amplifiers.
Even when the cause is actually multiplicative, it is not uncommon for the per-amp differences to be negligible for photometry but important for background estimation.

Background matching should excel at handling these features, and we can include them in the background model as simple per-amplifier pedestals.
A simple but expensive way of evaluating these in tract coordinates is to just warp an image of ones for each amplifier.
When a background-to-photometric ratio is also in play, however, we can just warp per-amplifier cutouts of inverse ratio image for effectively no additional cost.

\subsection{Degenerate Combinations}

A combined basis that has terms for amplifier pedestals and tract-coordinate splines is at least somewhat degenerate, because splines can represent amplifier pedestal exactly on any superpixels that are sufficiently far from an amplifier boundary.
This should be handled naturally by null-space projection, but it is possible the algorithm could be made more efficient by projecting out these degeneracies in the combined basis in advance.

When a background-to-photometric ratio is also in play, this degeneracy could actually be used to instead make basis evaluation more efficient.
If the features captured by the background-to-photometric ratio are small in amplitude and/or on scales larger than the splines, we could rely on the amplifier pedestals -- which can easily include the ratio, and are relatively few in number -- to soak up most of the background, so that we could skip dividing the spline basis by the ratio and restore the visit-independence of some of the basis.
